  linked in the unified deposit register.

 A further rapid entry sequence occurs on 27 July: at 08:47:14, 3f963 converts USDT into exactly 120 BNB (conversion). After
  75 seconds it deposits 100 BNB, followed by 10 + 10 BNB; the 120-BNB batch is complete by 08:49:14. This is funding inside
  the known depositor, not an identified Tornado withdrawal.

 What Tornado conceals

 The entry reveals its sender, pool, denomination and a commitment. A later exit reveals a recipient,
  relayer, fee and a different nullifier hash. The zero-knowledge proof establishes that a valid deposit exists
  without revealing which deposit it spends. The public commitment cannot simply be looked up against
  withdrawal hashes.

  The spent-note check requires the nullifier hash, not the commitment. Possessing these 22 public commitments therefore
  does not tell us which notes have been withdrawn or remain unspent. The relevant possible deposits are constrained by the
  same chain, pool, denomination and withdrawal proof root; they are not all transfers everywhere.

 Why this is a red flag here

 The observable choice was to reduce public traceability after token sales and liquidity removal. The
  repeated use, rapid April timing and common upstream links make authorization and destination records
  material questions. The 10/100-BNB splits follow the pools' fixed denominations; the split alone does not
  establish a separate concealment scheme. No recovered project notice explains the reason for these
  particular deposits.

  Reduced traceability is established; the operators' actual motive is not. The 1,210-BNB deposit sum includes mixed funding
  and is not an exclusive GMI-sale-proceeds or loss total. The separate July bridge remains transparently traceable and has not
  been identified as a Tornado exit.

  Primary protocol sources: core contract, withdrawal circuit, and pinned BSC deployment configuration. Raw captures and
  exact address comparisons are preserved.
